# Postdystopian Web

## I want to believe

The internet has been my second home for most of my life. I grew up deeply immersed in internet culture: online games, LAN parties, late-night chat rooms. I spent nearly all my free time there. That, most likely, is what led me into the profession I’ve been practicing for the past ten years. But what’s funny is that back then, my motivation wasn’t to build — it was to break. To hack games, find loopholes, bypass security, gain access to places I wasn’t supposed to be.

![Miracle of the Snow by Masolino da Panicale](<images/1 - Postdystopian Web/0*9M-aW5D_Sz9Tdtca.jpg> "*Miracle of the Snow* by Masolino da Panicale")

A long time has passed since then, and I see it differently. With the rise of AI, with this cosmic pace, comes a cosmic surge in threats. Not because people have become more malicious, but because the tools have become more accessible. Before, to cheat the system, you needed years of experience, ingenuity, and deep code knowledge. Now, you just feed a task to a language model — and it will pick the lock on any door in seconds. It’s like giving every schoolchild a master key. Not to one house — to all of them at once.

## Do you feel the turbulence?

We are standing on the threshold of a classic dystopia. The one where everything falls apart, where chaos and distrust become the norm, and every step requires proof that you’re not a bot or a fraud. Where security devolves into an endless war: we build walls, AI tears them down; we build them higher — it tears them down faster. This is a dead end, and it could very well become our reality.

But I see another scenario. Not a utopia — I don’t believe too much in utopias. But something that can be called a **postdystopia**. A state where we have survived the peak of the crisis, not broken, but rebuilt the rules of the game. Where the internet doesn’t die and doesn’t splinter into “safe” and “dangerous” zones, but transforms the very nature of interaction within itself.

Postdystopia is not the absence of threats. It’s the absence of the need to respond to them with personal fear and repression. It’s a shift from defense to infrastructure, from punishment to dialogue, from personal trust to protocols.

## What might this look like

With powerful language models at their disposal, people may indeed start breaking everything they can, wherever they can gain an advantage or an unconventional edge. If this becomes widespread, the game itself will collapse. When too many players break the rules, those who still play fairly gradually lose interest and drop out. The system is left without a stable core — and it fades, not because the technology has disappeared, but because the motivation to participate in it in its former form has vanished. This, by the way, goes far beyond the web itself.

But perhaps a state may emerge where people simply have no sustained interest in harming each other or stealing from one another in the conventional sense. And then the very concept of protection shifts: from defense and punishment, it becomes a form of conditional interaction.

If something goes wrong, it doesn’t necessarily lead to punitive measures or an external authority. Rather, it becomes a reason for dialogue: perhaps the person made a mistake, got confused, misunderstood. In this mode, conflict ceases to be a personal attack and becomes a situation of clarification.

## Millennial has invented trust

Here it’s important to clarify: the goal is not the absence of harm. The absence of harm is a baseline condition — the foundation on which movement is possible, but not the goal itself. We often confuse goals with means. One might say: my goal is to buy a car. But the car itself is not the goal. The goal is the ability to move, to transport, to change the state of the world. In one case, it’s sporty motion; in another, it’s utilitarian transport — for example, hauling one’s own horse. What matters is not ownership of the object, but the possibility it opens up.

The same applies to the internet and trust. We don’t need an “ideal digital friend.” We need money to transfer honestly, goods to arrive, and mistakes to be resolved through dialogue rather than litigation.

People act not through constant personal wariness, but by following clear behavioral patterns. And the human layer remains — the ability to deviate from formal logic if the situation calls for it.

> Without your trust, my flow breaks.

This is a shift from personal trust to infrastructural interaction. It’s not about morality — it’s about logic. And if we adopt this model, conflict doesn’t disappear, but it changes form. A mistake becomes not a reason for accusation, but a reason for adjusting the interaction scheme. Responsibility shifts from people to the schemes of interaction themselves.

## Postdystopia, huh?

Postdystopia is not a denial of complexity. On the contrary, it makes the internet more complex: new layers of moderation, verification, and reputation systems emerge. But this is a transition. And in this transition, what matters is not choosing the “right” model, but recognizing that the line between safe and dangerous may blur. But if we are careful with each other, if we stop dividing the world into “honest” and “crooks” and begin to see each participant as part of a shared system — then the internet can become not just a place, but a space of interaction.

Where access is for everyone, because everyone needs it.
Where trust is not a risk, but a baseline.

Not a utopia, not paradise, not the triumph of good over evil. It’s simply a world where we’ve stopped wasting energy chasing offenders, because we’ve learned to build roads that are hard to leave. And if I can take even one step so that another person doesn’t have to face this alone, amid the noise and fear — I will take it.
