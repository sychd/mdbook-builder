# Love That Does Not Blind

## On clarity, boundaries, and choosing to expand rather than contract

![The Sacred Heart of Jesus and the Crucifix](<images/0 - Chapter one/0*_ZTvrgHoS9xX4Yql.jpg>)

There is a common misconception: to love means to smooth things over. To close your eyes. To blindly follow the statements that differences don’t exist, that evil is a misunderstanding, and that any conflict can be dissolved with warmth.

But real love begins elsewhere.

It begins at the point where you see clearly. You see that the person in front of you may be causing harm. Lying. Acting from fear, aggression, or emptiness. You don’t excuse them. You don’t romanticise them. You don’t minimise the damage. You see — and you do not look away.

And at that point, you stop reducing the person to their actions. You can call them cruel, weak, destructive — but inside, you still understand that this does not define them entirely. That you are still facing a living being, confused, distorted, but not equal to their shadow. And you choose to love not their behaviour — but the sheer fact of their existence.

> This is not a manual for the moment of impact. If you are bleeding, tend to the wound first. If you are in acute pain, do not reach for philosophy — reach for safety, for rest, for someone who can hold the space while you cannot hold yourself. This text is for the quiet hours, for the training ground of ordinary days. It is here that you build the muscle that might — one day, if you are lucky — carry you through the storm. But no one builds muscle while the storm is still raging.

## Love as a stance, not an emotion

This is important: we are not talking about a feeling. Feelings come and go. Today you feel warmth — tomorrow you don’t. If you build on that, everything collapses easily.

What we are describing is closer to a choice. [Love as a fundamental orientation: not *against* someone, but *for* life itself](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2005/entries/friendship/#2). In this sense, anger, hatred, contempt — they are not “bad”, but they always narrow you down. They fix you at the point of conflict. Love expands you. It returns space to you. Not because it is “right”, but because it is more functional. It does not destroy you from within.

## The paradox: to love means to protect yourself

It is very important not to fall into naivety. [Love does not cancel boundaries](http://www.menningerclinic.org/blog/boundaries-are-a-love-language). If someone is destructive — you step back. If they threaten you — you defend yourself. If they cause harm — you can stop them. But inside this, there is no hatred as fuel. There is clarity and action.

It is like stopping an animal that is attacking — not because you hate it, but because you choose life. Your own — and in a sense, its life too.

## Returning to love as a practice

This is not a “once and for all” state. It is a return. You get angry — and you notice it. You judge — and you see it. You want to turn away — and you mark the moment. Then, if you have the resources, you make a small shift: you do not suppress, but you redirect your attention back to a wider perception. Not “I must love”, but “I can choose not to contract.”

## Where the subtle risks lie

To prevent this idea from becoming a trap, honesty is essential. First, substituting love with tolerance for harm: without boundaries, this becomes self-destruction. Love without protection is not love — it is self-abandonment. Second, [spiritual bypassing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_bypass): there is a risk of skipping over anger without living through it. But unprocessed anger does not disappear — it simply goes into the shadows and begins to run the show. Third, the illusion of “we are one” as the erasure of differences: yes, one can experience unity, but in physical reality, differences exist and they have consequences. Ignoring them means losing contact with the perceptioned world.

You see reality without softening, you do not reduce a being to its actions, you maintain inner openness, but you act firmly when necessary. And the strongest thing about this is not morality, but pragmatics: hatred destroys its bearer, while love keeps them whole.
